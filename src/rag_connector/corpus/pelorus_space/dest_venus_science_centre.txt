The Aphrodite Science Centre occupies Deck 4 of the resort station and is dedicated to the question that has defined modern astrobiology: is there life in the clouds of Venus? The centre presents the available evidence without advocacy and without conclusion. Pelorus Voyages takes no position on the question, and the exhibits are designed in consultation with active researchers on both sides of the debate.

The centrepiece exhibit is the phosphine detection of 2020. A team of astronomers reported the detection of phosphine — a gas associated with biological processes on Earth — in the Venusian atmosphere at concentrations that could not be easily explained by known abiotic chemistry. The announcement was significant because the conditions at the cloud deck altitude where phosphine was detected (around 50 to 60 kilometres) are, in terms of temperature and pressure, among the most Earth-like environments in the solar system outside Earth itself. The detection has since been scrutinised intensely; some follow-up analyses have confirmed a spectral feature consistent with phosphine at lower concentrations, others have found the original signal difficult to reproduce, and the debate remains unresolved in the published literature as of the most recent exhibits update.

The second major exhibit traces what would need to be true for life to exist in the Venusian atmosphere. The cloud droplets themselves are sulphuric acid — hostile to life as we understand it — but the hypothesis involves life not in the clouds but using the clouds: hypothetical microorganisms suspended in the atmosphere that have adapted to manage the acid environment. The exhibit explains why this is not as implausible as it first sounds and what the biochemical preconditions for such adaptation would be. It also explains what it would not explain, and why the hypothesis has critics.

The centre also houses a working atmospheric chemistry research station — a collaboration between the Pelorus Voyages Science Programme and two university partners — where samples of the plume material that coats the observation windows are periodically analysed. Visitors can observe the analysis process through a glass partition and speak with the researchers during scheduled public hours. The research station produces ongoing data that is incorporated into the exhibits in near-real-time; it is one of the few functioning research operations accessible to civilian visitors in the solar system.
