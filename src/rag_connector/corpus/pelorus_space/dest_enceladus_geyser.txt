Enceladus is the final destination on the Outer Reaches Tour and Grand Solar Tour before the return transit to Earth. It is a small, icy moon of Saturn — only 500 kilometres in diameter — with a global ocean of liquid water beneath its ice shell. The southern polar region vents this subsurface ocean into space through a series of active geysers that continuously spray water vapour, ice particles, and organic compounds into Saturn's outer ring system. The material from Enceladus's geysers feeds Saturn's E ring; the moon is literally building part of the ring system.

The Enceladus Geyser Flyover is a close approach to the southern plume field. The vessel passes at an altitude of approximately 25 kilometres above the surface — close enough to observe individual geyser vents and to pass through the outer fringe of the plume material. The plume is not dangerous; the material is fine ice particles and water vapour at very low density. However, it does coat the observation windows. Cleaning requires approximately four hours and is factored into the schedule; observation window visibility is typically reduced for the last hour of the approach and the beginning of the post-encounter transit.

There is no surface access at Enceladus. No EVA is conducted, and no gondola or equivalent descends. The encounter is entirely from the observation deck and the vessel's observation windows. This is a deliberate choice: Enceladus is one of the strongest candidate locations for extraterrestrial life in the solar system, and planetary protection protocols prohibit any contact between Earth-origin materials and the Enceladus surface or plume zone below a minimum altitude threshold. The 25-kilometre approach altitude is determined by these protocols, not by operational preference.

The plume field observation is accompanied by a narrated briefing from the vessel's science officer. The briefing covers the evidence for subsurface liquid water, the organic compound detections in the plume material, and the current scientific debate over whether the conditions in the Enceladus ocean could support microbial life. The science officer will also explain why the planetary protection protocols that prevent closer approach exist and what "biosignature candidate" means in practical terms for the astrobiological research community.

At the conclusion of the Enceladus encounter, the vessel departs for the return transit to Earth. The Enceladus stop is typically the emotionally significant close of the outer system leg — many passengers describe the silence of the return departure as a notable moment after weeks of active excursion scheduling.
